Image Compression via Wavelets, Fourier and PCA

Natural images are full of redundancy: project one onto a good basis, keep only the largest coefficients, and most of it survives. For Harvard AM205 (Fall 2025), Dylan Chua and Christina Wang compared three such bases, the discrete wavelet transform, the discrete Fourier transform and PCA. Try them on an image below.

02 — Motivation

Images live in a small subspace

Raw images are extremely high dimensional, yet most of those dimensions hold either noise that matters little to the eye or values nearly indistinguishable from their neighbours. A basic analysis of the CIFAR-10 (50000 images, 32×32) and STL-10 (5000 images, 96×96) datasets shows this two ways:

  1. Low rank. Flattening each image to a vector and running PCA on the dataset, the singular values decay exponentially, so natural images lie in a much lower-rank space than their raw dimension.
  2. Local correlation. The average covariance between two grayscale pixels is higher the closer they are, so pixels are more closely related to their neighbours than to distant ones.

Compression exploits this: decompose the image onto a suitable orthonormal (or biorthogonal) basis and keep only the important coefficients. JPEG uses the discrete cosine transform; JPEG 2000 replaced it with the discrete wavelet transform.

  1. Up to CR < 103, DWT is best, then DFT, then PCA

    This holds on both datasets and under both PSNR and SSIM. Wavelet coefficients are highly sparse, so a small fraction captures most of the image; the DFT is less sparse because it captures only global frequency structure.

  2. At very high CR, DFT overtakes DWT

    On the high-res dataset DWT quality drops sharply beyond CR ≈ 103. Heavily truncated wavelets keep only local changes, the edges, so the image looks almost binary, while the DFT keeps global low frequencies and gives a smoother, more recognisable picture.

05 — Conclusion

Which method to use

DWT is the best general choice

Low runtime complexity, sparse coefficients, the highest quality in most cases, and reconstructions that look better at reasonable CRs.

DFT wins when resources are scarce

It ran slightly faster in practice and gives the best quality when images must be compressed extremely hard.

PCA performs worst

Its runtime blows up at low CR, its metrics are the lowest, and even reasonable metrics hide poor reconstructions. Invaluable elsewhere, unsuitable for image compression.
